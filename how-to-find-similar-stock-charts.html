Chart pattern discovery

How to find similar stock charts using a reference pattern

Start with the setup you recognise, explore comparable chart structures and know what to check before trusting a match.

Crane Platform Labs · 6 November 2026 · Educational article

You spot an interesting setup: price tests the same resistance area several times, the pullbacks become shallower and the chart begins to tighten. How do you find other US stocks developing a similar pattern without building a complicated scanner?

That is the idea behind finding similar stock charts. Start with a visual reference, search for comparable chart structures, then review the results yourself. The goal is to discover candidates for further research—not to predict the next breakout.

Start with the chart pattern you recognise

Traditional stock scanners start with numeric rules such as volume, moving averages and price thresholds. Visual similarity search starts with a different question: what other charts look like this one? That can be especially useful when you can describe a setup by its shape more easily than by a list of filter settings.

Consider an ascending-triangle-style formation. The features you care about might be repeated tests of roughly the same resistance zone, progressively higher lows, and price compressing into a narrower range. A visual search can help you explore charts with comparable geometry, but it cannot prove that every result is a true ascending triangle.

Choose a useful reference

A reference does not have to be a perfectly annotated trading chart. Find Similar Charts is designed around three starting points:

  • A chart screenshot: use an example of the formation you want to find.
  • A simple sketch: communicate the shape you have in mind when you do not have an existing ticker to use as a reference.
  • A setup-library pattern: choose a predefined chart structure to begin a similarity search.

The reference library is for finding similar setups; it is not a source of charts for AI analysis. The AI Chart Analyzer is a separate workflow for charts supplied by the customer.

Compare the structure, not just the outline

Two charts can look alike at a glance while representing very different situations. Once results appear, inspect the details that matter to the original idea.

  • Resistance: are the highs concentrated near a meaningful price area, or is the resemblance superficial?
  • Higher lows: do buyers appear to be stepping in at progressively higher levels?
  • Pattern maturity: is the stock still consolidating, approaching a breakout, or already extended beyond the reference?
  • Time frame: compare setups over similar periods; a formation on a daily chart is not interchangeable with one on a five-minute chart.
  • Volume and context: check whether participation, liquidity and relevant company news change the interpretation.

A practical way to research the results

First, select a clear screenshot, sketch or library reference. Second, review the closest matching charts for the structural elements you actually intended to find. Third, open the candidates that look convincing and inspect their price history, volume and technical context. Finally, compare the result with the original reference and decide whether it warrants more research.

It is reasonable to reject most results. Similarity search is a discovery tool; a high visual resemblance is not a quality score, a recommendation, or evidence that a price move is likely to repeat.

What if a search produces no convincing match?

That can be a useful outcome. Your reference may be unusually specific, the supported market universe may not currently contain a close equivalent, or the pattern may need to be simplified. Try a clearer reference that emphasises the two or three features you actually care about. Do not weaken your standards simply to make a result look attractive.
