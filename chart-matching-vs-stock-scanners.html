Stock scanners vs chart matching: two different ways to find setups

Crane Platform Labs · 4 November 2026 · Educational article

Most stock research tools begin with filters. But sometimes traders start with a picture: a chart that looks interesting and a question about what else is forming a similar shape.

What a stock scanner does well

A traditional scanner takes a set of conditions—perhaps trading volume, a moving average or a technical breakout rule—and checks them against a list of securities. If the rules are precise, the results are consistent and repeatable.

That makes scanning useful when the researcher already knows which measurable conditions matter. It can also expose new candidates across a large market universe. The weakness is the interface: technical filters may be hard to express, and a result can satisfy the numbers while looking quite different from the chart a person had in mind.

What chart matching changes

Visual matching reverses the workflow. Instead of building a long filter list, the user starts with a reference chart and looks for other securities with similar structures. The question changes from “Which stocks meet these thresholds?” to “What else resembles this setup?”

A pattern search could be helpful for exploring tight bases, higher lows, resistance compression or other recurring formations. But a similarity score is not a forecast. Two charts can look alike and still behave differently because of volume, broader market conditions or new information.

Which approach is better?

Neither one replaces the other. Scanners are strong at applying known conditions consistently; chart matching is strong at expressing the visual structure that catches a trader's eye. A useful workflow can combine both: use a scanner to discover candidates, then use a reference chart to explore related setups.

A practical example

Imagine a chart with multiple tests of a resistance area and a series of rising lows. A scanner might identify securities with certain price and volume properties. A chart-matching search instead asks which historical shapes resemble that particular formation. The researcher still needs to inspect each result and decide whether the pattern is meaningful.

How this informs our products

We built EdgeBreak around market scanning, chart research and technical context. We're developing Find Similar Charts as a more direct, chart-first experience. The two approaches solve related but distinct research problems.

The important feature is not that software declares a chart “good”. It is that people can discover interesting structures, inspect the evidence and make their own decisions.
